Teen savings accounts have quietly become one of the most competitive corners of consumer banking, and New York families have more good choices than ever — from credit unions with sweepstakes-style savings incentives to national banks with polished apps. This guide is for parents and guardians in the Empire State who want an account that a 13-to-17-year-old can actually use, learn from, and grow with, without paying for the privilege. We evaluated 23 accounts on fees, yield, parental controls, branch and ATM reach, and how well each one teaches real money habits.

What to Consider

Age rules and custodial structure. Most institutions split the teen years into two buckets: a custodial or joint account for ages 13–17 that a parent co-owns, and a standalone account the teen can hold alone at 18. Some New York credit unions require a parent to be a member first — a five-minute step that unlocks better rates. Read the fine print on who legally owns the balance, because it affects taxes and what happens if the teen wants to move the money later.

Yield and fee structure. A teen account with a $5 monthly service fee and 0.01% APY is a lesson in the wrong direction. Look for no monthly maintenance fee, no minimum balance, and an APY that at least keeps pace with a high-yield savings account. On a $2,000 balance, the gap between 0.05% and 3.00% is roughly $59 a year — real money for a teenager saving toward a first car or a semester abroad.

Access and controls. The best accounts give teens a debit card and a mobile app while giving parents the ability to set spending limits, lock a lost card, and get real-time transaction alerts. In New York, where a teen might tap a MetroCard, order lunch on a phone, and split a cab on the same Saturday, instant card controls matter more than a paper statement ever did.

New York practicality. Branch density varies wildly by borough, county, and upstate region. If your family is in Buffalo, Albany, or Syracuse, a credit union with local branches and shared ATM networks beats a branchless app. If you're in Manhattan or Brooklyn, surcharge-free ATM access through the CO-OP or Allpoint networks can matter more than a physical lobby.

Habits and incentives. The accounts that actually change behavior tend to have a hook: automatic transfers from a parent's account, a savings challenge with prizes, or a goal-setting tool built into the app. A teen who watches a balance climb toward a named goal — concert tickets, a laptop, a first car — is far more likely to keep saving after the novelty wears off.

How We Selected

We started with 23 accounts available to New York residents, then cut anything with a monthly maintenance fee that couldn't be waived, anything requiring a minimum opening deposit above $25, and anything without a mobile app rated reasonably well by users. From there we scored each account on five weighted criteria: total cost of ownership over three years, APY competitiveness, parental control features, ATM and branch access in New York, and educational or incentive programming. We also weighed how easy it is to open an account online versus in person, since a 45-minute branch visit is a genuine barrier for a working parent. Nine accounts survived. Broadview took the top spot on the strength of its savings incentive program and fee-free structure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a teenager open a savings account in New York without a parent?

In most cases, no. New York institutions generally require an adult co-owner or custodian for accounts held by anyone under 18. A handful of credit unions allow teens as young as 13 to hold a sub-account under a parent's primary membership. Once your teen turns 18, they can convert to a standalone account at most of the institutions listed here.

How much money should a teen keep in a savings account versus a checking account?

A useful rule of thumb: keep one to two months of typical spending in checking for day-to-day card use, and move everything else into savings. If your teen is saving toward a specific goal, automate a transfer on payday so the savings happens before the spending does. Accounts with a built-in budgeting tool make this easier to see at a glance.

Are credit unions or banks better for teen savings in New York?

Credit unions typically win on fees and rates, and several offer savings incentive programs that banks don't match. Banks typically win on branch and ATM convenience and app polish. The right answer depends on whether your family values a local branch or a better return — and there's no rule against using one for spending and the other for saving.

What documents do I need to open a teen savings account?

Expect to provide your own government-issued photo ID, your Social Security number, your teen's Social Security number and birth certificate or passport, and proof of a New York address such as a utility bill or lease. Credit unions may also ask for proof of membership eligibility. Many institutions now let you upload everything during online onboarding.

The Bottom Line

Whichever you choose, open it with your teenager sitting next to you, set up an automatic transfer, and name a goal. The account matters less than the habit.
